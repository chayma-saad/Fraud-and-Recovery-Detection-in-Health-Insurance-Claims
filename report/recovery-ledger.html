01 EXHIBIT A · DATA PROCESSING

From six workbooks to one ledger

Four claims workbooks and five beneficiary workbooks, each with title rows above the real header and a broken beneficiary join key, get reduced to one clean table — then to a severe imbalance no accuracy score can hide.

Cleaning funnel · rows remaining at each stage

Bars are drawn to true scale. The post-termination step is nearly invisible on purpose — only 9 of over a million rows. The missing-core-field step is where 17.6% of the dataset is genuinely lost, not imputed.

Recovery vs. everything else · true scale

1 in every 1,367 claims was later flagged for recovery. A classifier that predicts "never recoverable" for everything scores 99.93% accuracy while catching none of them — which is why every model here is judged on PR‑AUC and recall at a review threshold, never accuracy.

Seventeen and a half percent of the raw dataset is discarded outright — not imputed, not guessed — because a claim missing its amount, date, status, or diagnosis is not a case a recovery model can learn from. notebooks/01_data_processing.ipynb · docs/01_data_processing.md
02 EXHIBIT B · RECOVERY‑RISK MODEL

Ranking risk, not guessing fraud

Four candidates, one time-based split (train ≤2014, validate 2015, test on the untouched 2016 year) so no model ever learns from claims that hadn't happened yet.

Model comparison · 2016 test set

XGBoost wins on PR‑AUC (ranking quality across every threshold); logistic regression wins on Recall@5% (71.4%) — the actual audit-queue operating point. Neither wins outright, and the thesis reports both rather than flattening it into one number.

§

The business number: reviewing just the riskiest 5% of 2016's claims (2,494 of 49,876) would have caught 15 of 28 real recoveries — AED 10,803 of AED 20,186 in claimed-amount exposure on those claims (53.5%).

The single strongest feature isn't the peer-deviation score the model was built around — it's whether the provider is in Abu Dhabi. Non-Abu-Dhabi providers show a 2–17× higher recovery rate, every year, including the untouched 2016 test year. notebooks/02_modeling.ipynb · docs/02_modeling.md
03 EXHIBIT C · ADVERSARIAL RED‑TEAM

Teaching an AI to cheat, so we can stop it

A PPO agent is dropped into a closed, synthetic claims simulator (CTGAN-generated — no real patient data enters the sandbox) with one goal: extract as much of a fraud budget as possible without a static XGBoost defender flagging it.

Attack success rate · same agent, before / after one retraining round

337 evasive claims the agent discovered were folded into the defender's training data. The agent itself was never retrained — this is the same frozen policy, scored against a defender that has now seen its tricks.

How the agent spent its fraud budget · 271 successful episodes

Of the splitting minority: 10 episodes used 2 claims, 5 used 3, 2 used 4, and 10 used the full 5-claim maximum. Splitting is a fallback, not the primary strategy — brute-force amount inflation already works against a defender with no peer-group context.

A random policy already wins 86% of the time. The real finding isn't that an AI learned to cheat — it's how little defense there was to begin with, and how cheaply one retraining round closed the gap. notebooks/03_adversarial_selfplay.ipynb · docs/03_adversarial_selfplay.md
04 SYNTHESIS

What's proven, what's next

This is a scoped MVP at every phase — deliberately, and documented as such rather than presented as more finished than it is.

Proven

  • Time-based split beats every baseline without leaking the future
  • XGBoost / LightGBM both clear PR‑AUC 0.50 vs. a 0.002 peer-score floor
  • A weak defender's amount-inflation blind spot is real and quantifiable
  • One retraining round on 337 examples cuts attack success by 75%

Explicitly out of scope

  • Full multi-round co-evolution loop (this MVP ran one round)
  • PettingZoo two-sided multi-agent environment
  • LLM tool-calling fraudster agent
  • Peer-deviation-aware defender inside the RL simulator
